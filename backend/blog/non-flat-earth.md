A few years ago, my good friend Manu proposed to me a fun challenge to which I'm still committed today. Amid the [flat-earth fever of 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_flat_Earth_beliefs#Internet-era_resurgence)
, he was looking for a strong, convincing experiment to disprove planar Earth to the eyes of honestly skeptical thinkers. I was studying physics back then, so I was a natural choice for him to share the task.


I started crafting the requirements for an honest-flat-earther-proof experiment. First, the experimental method should be trustless: no third parties to rely on: no people nor encyclopaedic prior knowledge. Secondly, the method should be easy to implement, to reach the greatest number of honest skeptical individuals. This means low-cost, low-knowledge, low-time demand. Below is my final requirements list aiming to capture this trustless-Do-It-Yourself policy:


1) Doable by a single person
2) No assumptions of the universe shape and distances
3) No need for specialized training (math, physics, sophisticated instrumentation)
4) Low budget
5) Short preparation time


For instance, I shall discard the [Eratosthenes measurement of Earth's radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_circumference#Eratosthenes), because it assumes a far away Sun (2), and requires some grasp of trigonometry (3). Also, you'd need to measure the distance between two distant cities by yourself. Not to mention that you'd also have to measure the angles of shadows within each city at the same time without collaboration (1), and use trigonometry to draw conclusions (3).


After some careful thought, a promising heuristic came up. There should be a smart way to leverage the [topological properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology) of a rotating closed surface, in order to clearly distinguish it from a rotating plane. You see, for us human visual creatures, many topological properties are deeply intuitive, and require no arithmetic or trigonometry. Let's look after these deep visual intuitions able to put on the table sensible evidence that there is something different happening on each hemisphere, and that this difference couldn't manifest on a flat Earth.


In popular culture, there is this creatively funny [episode of the Simpsons](https://simpsons.fandom.com/wiki/Bart_vs._Australia) referencing the [Coriolis force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_force), which is a nice instance of a topological property of a rotating sphere having opposite effects on each side. In this particular episode, Bart calls people in the southern and northern hemispheres to check if the toilet's flushed water rotated in opposite directions on each side of the planet. Although the episode exaggerates Coriolis force effect - it couldn't be detected at short scales such as a toilet - Coriolis does cause remarkable effects over oceanic flows, shaping opposed flowing patterns on each hemisphere.


But oceanic flows are too large and too complex for a single person to measure and interpret with a constrained budget and knowledge. Let's change the scope upwards to the sky: the stars also "flow". Their movement is special because they describe concentric circles by keeping the distances between them in a careful choreography. If you check images of star trails you'll immediately notice they rotate with perfect synchrony.

How do stars come to agree to move altogether? Are they stuck into an immense crystal sphere which rotates? Are we actually rotating, instead? For the experiment I'm about to propose, the difference doesn't matter, and we shall not rely on encyclopedic knowledge, so you figure that by yourself. By the way, rotation is a nice kind of movement worth thinking about (which we did in our [previous post](https://igaray.com/blog/spinning)!)


The key insight here is that star paths have a very intuitive and differentiating property, able to distinguish flat surfaces against round surfaces. Why? Because if the Earth was completely flat, then everyone should see the stars rotating in the same direction. Conversely, if the Earth was round (or any closed surface of your preference), then opposed hemispheres should see opposing sky rotations. Any observer should see the stars changing their rotation direction as soon as they cross to the other hemisphere. The gif below is a visual helper illustrating the phenomenon:

![alt text](/static/coupled_disks_perspective_midpoint.gif)

In the animation, both disks rotate coupled. They represent far away stars moving all together with synchrony, keeping their distances. An observer standing on the right pole of the blue dot would see the right disk rotating counter-clockwise. However, the same observer standing on the left pole, would see the left disk rotating clockwise. It's the perspective that changes depending on which side of the blue dot we stand. When we flip the perspective from left to right (or south to north in Earth's case), the rotation perspective also flips. In a flat earth, there is only one side. It is impossible to flip the perspective, so stars should always maintain the same rotation.

Hence, recording stars movement is a nice experiment, because anyone can perform it by themselves, using just their naked eyes (or cellphone or filming device), from each hemisphere. I acknowledge the budget to cross hemispheres in a roundtrip (around 500 USD perhaps?) is not as inclusive as I'd liked, but it's absolutely achievable during most people's lifetime.
